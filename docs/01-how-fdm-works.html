Layers and lines

FDM (fused deposition modelling) squeezes molten plastic through a nozzle and draws it in thin layers. With a standard 0.4 mm nozzle the extruded line is a bit wider than the hole — about 0.42–0.45 mm — and the layer height is usually 0.08–0.28 mm (0.20 is the everyday default). Thin layers give smooth curves and slow prints; thick layers give fast, rough prints.

layers = model_height / layer_height
loops  = wall_thickness / line_width      (round to a whole number)

The big physical truths

1. Parts are weaker between layers

Each layer bonds to the one below it by heat, which is weaker than the plastic running inside a single line. A part is strongest along the lines and weakest when pulled across layers (the Z axis). Orientation is a strength decision.

2. Gravity wins on overhangs

A layer needs something underneath it. Up to roughly 45° of lean from vertical, each layer is supported mostly by the previous one. Beyond that the underside gets rough and, near horizontal, needs support. Flat gaps crossed by a bridge sag more the longer they are.

3. Plastic shrinks as it cools

Large flat footprints in materials such as ABS or ASA can peel at the corners (warping). This is why the X2D's heated chamber matters (module 7), and why sharp, large base corners are risky.

4. The first layer is squished on purpose

That squish produces the elephant foot: a slight bulge at the bottom edge. Chamfer the bottom edge of precision parts (module 4).

Worked example: a hook, two ways

A wall hook has an arm that bends under load. Print it standing up and the layer lines cross the arm: it snaps along a layer. Print it lying flat and the layers run along the arm: it bends and holds. Same STL, same settings, very different part. The lying-flat version needs a flat face on the plate, so you may redesign the hook so one side is flat.

Exercises

EXERCISE 1

Count the layers

A 48 mm tall part is printed at 0.16 mm, 0.20 mm and 0.28 mm layer height. How many layers each?

Hint
Divide height by layer height.
Show solution
48/0.16 = 300 layers; 48/0.20 = 240 layers; 48/0.28 ≈ 171 layers. Going from 0.16 to 0.28 cuts layers by about 43%, which is roughly why print time drops, though walls, infill and travel also matter.
EXERCISE 2

How many wall loops?

You want about 2.0 mm walls with a 0.4 mm nozzle (line ≈ 0.42 mm). How many wall loops should the slicer use?

Hint
Divide 2.0 by 0.42.
Show solution
2.0 / 0.42 ≈ 4.8, so use 4 loops (≈1.7 mm) or 5 loops (≈2.1 mm). Designing walls as multiples of the line width avoids gap-fill artefacts.
EXERCISE 3

Orient the part

A phone stand has a thin angled back plate that carries the phone's weight. Should the layers run along the plate's length or across its thickness? Describe how to place it on the build plate.

Hint
Where does the load try to pull layers apart?
Show solution
Layers should run along the plate's length so the bending load is along the lines. Place the stand lying on its back or side so the long plate is parallel to the build plate. Never print it standing on its thin edge.

Checkpoint quiz

A 0.4 mm nozzle produces a line about…

Lines are slightly wider than the nozzle because the plastic is squished against the layer below.

A printed part is weakest…

Inter-layer bonding is weaker than continuous extruded plastic.

Which overhang prints best without supports?

45° is a widely used limit where each layer still sits mostly on the one below.

The 'elephant foot' is caused by…

Squish on the early layers bulges the bottom edge; a 0.4–0.6 mm chamfer hides it.

3 loops on a 0.42 mm line give a wall of about…

3 × 0.42 ≈ 1.26 mm.
